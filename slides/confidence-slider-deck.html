SayKnowing includes knowing how sure to be. An assistant that says it is 99% sure and is wrong misleads its user, because the stated confidence is often all the user has. This project asks where that confidence comes from when a small model is fine-tuned on a larger model's answers.

Small models are often fine-tuned on a larger model's answers. Its confidence travels with them. What does the student do with it?

1,000 held-out TriviaQA questions, greedy answer (paper, Table A).

SayOn the same thousand trivia questions, eighteen hosted models all state about 95 to 99% confidence, whether they are right 92% of the time or 40%. I measure this with one number, the overconfidence gap: stated confidence minus accuracy, in points. Zero is the target.

Same answers, four confidence labels

Teacher DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, 90% right. Students Qwen3.5-9B and Granite-4.1-8B, about 60% right. 1,978 records, 24 students trained.

Only the confidence number differs: the teacher's own, calibrated for the teacher, fitted to the student, or shuffled. Predictions were committed before the runs.

SayThe design is a label-only test. One large teacher writes the training answers for two small students. The answers stay identical. Only the confidence number in the records changes: the teacher's own, the teacher's after calibration, a value fitted to the student's own accuracy, or the calibrated values shuffled across questions. Then the students answer a thousand questions they have never seen.

SayResult one. The records work like a slider. Move the confidence in the records, and the student's stated confidence follows, from 55 to 98%. How often it is right hardly moves: about 60% at every setting. In the example the student gives the same wrong composer each time, once with 40%, once with 99%. The teacher's knowledge hardly came along: about three of the 32 points between teacher and student.

Why calibrating the teacher is not enough. The gap splits into three parts: copying error, label gap, accuracy difference. Teacher's own: +40.1 = −0.3 + 8.1 + 32.3. Calibrated teacher: +31.2 = −1.4 − 0.4 + 33.1. Calibration removes the label gap. The accuracy difference between teacher and student stays.
SayResult two. The obvious safeguard is to calibrate the teacher first. It is not enough. With the teacher's own confidence the students are overconfident by about 40 points. With a calibrated teacher, still by 31 and 27. The reason: 90% is honest for a teacher that is right nine times in ten. The student is right six times in ten and repeats the same number. Set the confidence against the student's own accuracy, and most of the gap is gone.

Usable for a decision. Answering only the more confident half of the questions, the students were right on 75% and 80% of them; with shuffled labels 60% and 62% (post hoc, sampled answer).

Filled: Qwen3.5-9B. Ring: Granite-4.1-8B. Shuffling keeps the level and destroys which question got which value.

SayResult three is the good news. The teacher's confidence also carries which questions are hard. Students trained on it can tell their right answers from their wrong ones, on questions they have never seen. Shuffle the same numbers across the records and that ability is gone. If these students answer only the more confident half of the questions, they are right on 75 and 80% of them, against about 60% with shuffled labels. So a large model can pass its sense of difficulty to a small one.

What follows

  • Report the gap beside accuracy.How sure a fine-tuned model sounds is evidence of its training records, not of its knowledge.
  • Judge the student, not the teacher.Measure the student's own gap on questions it was not trained on. If nothing is done, expect about the accuracy difference between the two.
  • Keep the teacher's ranking, set the level on the student.Refit the labels to the student before training, or recalibrate it afterwards on held-out questions.
A design tool, and a risk. Confidence can be set for a task without paying in accuracy. The same slider can make a sales bot sound sure of what it barely knows. A stated confidence tells us first what the trainer put in, and only then what the model knows.

The application I find most interesting: a small model that cannot do everything but is aware of what it can do.

Limits, and what failed

  • A first step.One task (short-answer trivia), one large teacher, two students, LoRA on 7 to 9B models, 2,000 training records. One seed per cell, except four configurations with three.
  • Exploratory by the protocol's own rule.The lead experiment was added after the protocol's stopping time. Its predictions were still committed before the first teacher record.
  • Untested.Whether confidence carried only by wording, without a number, transfers the same way.
  • Three early predictions failedin the supporting experiments: that training damps a gap, that the greedy map forecasts the chains, and that overconfident students resist pushback more.
  • One registered range missed under a second grader.It put the fitted students at −7.4 and −7.9, outside the registered 7 points.
